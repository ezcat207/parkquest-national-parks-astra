How many national parks have I been to?

It is a harder question than it sounds. Most people remember the headline trips — the week in Yosemite, the drive through Zion — and forget the afternoon detour to Hot Springs or the ferry to Channel Islands. The counter above answers it properly: tick every park you have set foot in and it keeps a running total out of 63, the percentage you have completed, and how many are still waiting.

Your count is stored in this browser only. There is no account to create and nothing to sign in to, so the number is yours alone. If you switch phones or clear your browsing data, export a backup first from your passport and import it on the other device.

What counts as a national park?

The National Park Service looks after more than 400 places, but only 63 of them carry the full national park designation. That is the list this counter uses. National monuments, historic sites, seashores, battlefields and recreation areas are all part of the wider park system, and many are wonderful, but they are counted separately and are not included in the 63.

A few of the 63 surprise people. Gateway Arch in St. Louis is the smallest and sits in the middle of a city. Wrangell-St. Elias in Alaska is larger than Switzerland. The National Park of American Samoa sits in the South Pacific, and Virgin Islands National Park is in the Caribbean — so a genuinely complete collection involves two long flights most travellers never make.

What a realistic pace looks like

Sixty-three is a lifetime list, not a year-long sprint. Eight of them are in Alaska and four more are on islands, which means the overwhelming majority of visitors top out somewhere in the thirties and forties. Reaching ten already puts you ahead of most people you know. Utah and California are the efficient places to build a number quickly: Utah has five within a day's drive of each other, and California has nine.

If the count is what motivates you, group your trips by region rather than chasing single parks. The regional bars above show where you already have momentum, which is usually the cheapest place to add the next few.

The parks people miscount

Three mistakes come up again and again. The first is counting a national monument or historic site as a national park — understandable, since the signs look almost identical, but Muir Woods, Death Valley's neighbours and dozens of other beloved places sit outside the 63. The second is Sequoia and Kings Canyon: they are managed jointly by the Park Service and share an entrance, but they are two separate national parks and count as two.

The third is driving through without stopping. Whether a highway crossing counts is entirely up to you — there is no official rule and nobody is checking. Most collectors settle on a personal standard early, usually something like getting out of the car and spending real time there, and then apply it consistently. The number only means something if it means the same thing each time you add to it.

Counting with other people

Couples and families often want a shared number as well as individual ones. Because everything here is stored in your own browser, the simplest approach is to keep one device as the shared record and export a backup occasionally. Some people keep a personal count and a joint count separately — the parks you have seen, and the parks you have seen together. They are usually not the same list, and the gap between them tends to become the plan for the next few years.
